# What's new in the app

*2 November 2026*

Thirteen things went in today. None of them need anything from you to start
working, except the two with switches, which are described at the end.

---

## 1. Renters can sign things, properly

Everyone who signs in — you included — now has a **signature** kept on their
account. You add it once. You can draw it with a finger on a phone, or with a
mouse, or, if neither of those produces anything you recognise as your own
handwriting, **take a photo of your signature on paper and upload that**. The
app lifts the signature off the paper so it appears as ink on the page rather
than as a photograph of a bit of paper.

After that, whenever somebody signs something in the app, their signature goes
on it along with the date and the time.

### Why this is worth having

If a tenant ever disputes something they agreed to, this is what settles it.
Every signing records who signed, exactly what words they were agreeing to,
when, and which device it came from. Four things protect it, and all four are
built into the database rather than being promises the app makes:

- **Only the person themselves can sign.** You cannot sign on a tenant's
  behalf, even from your own admin account. The database refuses it. This is
  deliberate — it is the thing that makes a signature mean something.
- **What they signed is fixed.** The exact wording is fingerprinted at the
  moment of signing. If the document were altered afterwards, the fingerprint
  would no longer match, and that is provable either way.
- **Nothing can be edited or deleted afterwards.** Not by you, not by Tyler,
  not by anyone with full access to the database.
- **Each signing is linked to the one before it**, so if a record were ever
  removed from the middle, it would show.

It also records whether the signature was drawn on their own device or
uploaded as a picture, because those aren't equally strong evidence, and
whoever reads it later should be told which rather than left to assume.

One honest limit: this is good evidence, not a notarised document. It proves
control of an email inbox and a device, not identity.

## 2. Ending a tenancy is now a record instead of a memory

This was the part most likely to end in an argument about money, and it all
lived in texts and recollection.

**What the tenant sees.** As soon as you start a move-out, the clean-out list
appears in their account: seventeen jobs grouped by room, each saying what is
actually being asked — "oven, hob and extractor cleaned", with a note that it
is the one most often missed. They tick things off as they go; it saves
itself, there is no Save button to forget. They can add a note to any line.

They can sign the list **with things still unticked**. That's on purpose. A
list that pushes someone into ticking untrue boxes so they can get out of the
building is worse than no list. If they sign with four jobs undone, the signed
document says so, plainly, in their own words — which is far more useful to
you than a tidy list of lies.

They also give you a forwarding address for the deposit and any post.

**What you see.** Their list beside your verdict, line by line. For each line:
what they claimed, what you found, a note, and a cost if there's one. Then the
walk-through date and your notes. Then the deposit.

**The deposit is a ledger.** Not a number you keep adjusting, but a list of
movements: what was taken at the start, each deduction with a reason and a
receipt, and what went back. The balance is added up from those every time, so
it can't quietly stop matching its own history. The app won't let you close a
move-out while the balance is anything other than nil — "settled" with money
still showing is how a landlord ends up paying it twice.

When you do settle it, the tenancy is marked ended for you.

## 3. Profile pictures

Everyone can add a picture of themselves, you included. After choosing a photo
you can **drag it around and zoom in** until the right part of it is in the
circle — what you see in the circle is exactly what everyone else will see.

This is groundwork for the message threads we'll build next. A name on its own
reads as paperwork; a face reads as a person.

Your own picture and signature are both on your account page — there's a link
to it at the bottom of the menu down the left-hand side.

## 4. A note each morning  *(switch)*

A short email, first thing, with the things that actually need you that day:

- which repairs are waiting, and how long each has been waiting
- who is in the building today, and anything still unconfirmed
- any tenancy that runs out in the next ninety days
- where each move-out has got to
- what is overdue, what falls due today, what came in yesterday

Two things about it. **Nothing arrives on a quiet morning** — an email that
says "nothing to report" every day is one you stop reading, and the morning it
matters you'd delete it with the rest. And the test for whether something goes
in it is whether it changes what you do *today*. A total that's been the same
for a fortnight doesn't. A lease quietly ending in six weeks does, because six
weeks is roughly how long it takes to find the next tenant.

A repair nobody has acknowledged for a week is flagged as urgent, because that
is how a tenant decides not to bother reporting the next one.

**It's already on.** To stop it: Settings → "Your morning note".

## 5. Late fees  *(switch — off)*

You can now have the app add a fee to an invoice that stays unpaid. It is
**off**, and it stays off until you turn it on.

Please read this bit before you do. **In Alberta a late fee can only be
charged if it's written into the agreement the tenant signed.** If it isn't in
theirs, put it in the next one rather than charging it now. The settings page
says this too, so you don't have to remember it.

If you do turn it on, you choose the days of grace, a flat amount or a
percentage, and a ceiling. Then:

- a fee is added **once per invoice** — never again the next month on the same
  debt, which is the thing that turns one missed payment into a hole nobody
  can climb out of
- it's never more than what's owed
- it appears as its own small invoice, so the tenant can see exactly what it
  is and what it was for

Settings → "When an invoice goes unpaid".

## 6. A page for looking after the building

A new page, **The building**, with three things that have been living in your
head and nowhere else.

**Jobs that come round.** Gutters, the furnace, smoke alarms — what the job is,
how often, and when it's next due. Tick one off and the next one is counted
from the day you actually did it, not the day it was due. (So a furnace
serviced two months late is next due twelve months after *that*, rather than
ten — otherwise being late once makes every year afterwards slightly wrong.)

**Who you call.** Your tradespeople by trade, with the numbers tappable so you
can ring them from your phone, and a line for how it went last time in your own
words. More use in a year than a star rating. Taking somebody off the list
hides them rather than deleting them, so the repair history still says who did
the work.

**What's still under warranty**, with a photo of the paperwork attached. This
is the one that saves real money. A furnace with eight years of cover left is
worth nothing on the morning it stops if nobody can find the card.

There's also **what each room has cost** — every repair ever reported, grouped
by room. Anything reported more than once is called out, because the third time
the same radiator comes up the question stops being who can fix it.

Jobs that are late and cover about to run out both turn up in your morning
note.

## 7. Statements of account

Everything charged and everything paid, in date order, with a running balance.
A PDF you can attach to an email.

This is for the conversation that starts "I thought I'd paid that". There's a
**Statement** link beside each tenant on the Rentals page, and tenants can
get their own — this year or last year — from their account page.

Every statement ends by saying that if paying is difficult they should say so.
Not only when somebody asks: a person who's struggling usually says nothing
unless they're invited to.

---

## 8. Which rooms actually pay

On the Finance page: each room's year. What came in, what was spent on it,
what was left, and **how many days it stood empty and what those days cost**.

That last column is the one worth looking at. A room that earns well but sits
empty four months a year can be worth less than a cheaper one that never does,
and nothing in the app showed that before.

"Spent on it" is only money genuinely attached to that room — repairs, and any
expense you tag to it. Insurance, heating and the accountant belong to the
whole building and aren't divided up, because splitting them by floor size
gives a number that looks precise and tells you nothing.

## 9. A waiting list

On the Rentals page. Anyone who asks about a room goes on it, whether or not
anything is free at the time, with what would suit them and what they can pay.

When a room does come free, the people it would suit are marked. It isn't
choosing for you — it's making sure the right two names are in front of you
that morning instead of in a notebook. An empty room costs its rent every
month it's empty, and knowing who to ring is the only thing that shortens
that.

## 10. One screen for a new renter

A new page: **Rentals → Move somebody in**. Everything about a new renter in
one go, rather than scattered across four places.

Who they are, which room, when it starts, the rent, the deposit — and then the
things you'd otherwise write on an envelope: the emergency contact, how many
keys you handed over, their car and parking stall, who else is living there.

Picking a room fills in the rent and deposit from its asking price. Picking
somebody off the waiting list fills in their name and number and takes them
off the list. Only empty rooms are offered, so you can't accidentally put two
people in one.

At the end it offers to raise their first rent invoice and send them a welcome
email with their sign-in link. Both ticked by default.

None of those extra details are wanted on the day, which is exactly why they
get skipped — and every one of them is wanted at an awkward moment later. So
the emergency contact now shows on the room list in red, where you'd actually
look for it at eleven at night. The key count and the car come back up at the
walk-through when somebody leaves, next to the clean-out list.

## 11. Photograph a receipt instead of typing it

On the Finance page, when you go to record a cost, there's now a **Take a
photo of the receipt** button. Point your phone at the receipt and the boxes
fill themselves in — date, who it was paid to, the total, the GST, how you
paid, and what it was for.

**Nothing is saved until you press the button.** It fills the form and stops.
Check the total and the date against the paper, then save as you would have
anyway.

It will also tell you what it *couldn't* read rather than guessing. Tested on
a receipt torn off above the total, it left the date, total and tax blank and
said why — including that "10/11/2026" could mean the 10th of November or the
11th of October, so it wouldn't choose.

It picks from the categories you already use, so things get filed your way.

## 12. What the next six months look like

On the Finance page. Rent coming in from current tenancies, invoices already
raised, your regular costs going out, and what's left in the account at the
end of each month.

Two things make it worth trusting:

**A tenancy that ends stops paying.** If a room empties in March, March is
where it shows — which is the only reason to look ahead at all.

**It's cautious about costs.** Only things you've actually paid in at least
three of the last six months count as regular, at the middle figure rather
than the average, so one big repair never becomes a monthly expectation. And
nothing hoped for is in it — no grant that might come, no room that might let.
A forecast with hopes in it is a wish.

It needs one number nobody can look up for you: **what's actually in the
account**. Type it in at the top and the rest becomes useful. It's dated, and
after six weeks the page tells you it's gone stale rather than quietly
pretending.

## 13. A record of what changed

A new page: **What changed**. Every change to the money and the tenancies —
who made it, and when. Rent went from $850 to $890, a cost was added, a late
fee was charged overnight.

Changes the app made on its own are listed too, as "the app, on its own" —
the overnight rent run, a payment arriving from Stripe. Those are the hardest
to account for afterwards, which is exactly why they're there.

**It can't be edited or deleted by anyone**, including Tyler, including
anything with full access to the database. A record that can be tidied up
isn't a record.

**Counselling notes are deliberately not in it.** They're health information,
and a second copy sitting in a list meant to be read later is precisely what
the law is there to prevent.

## One thing that was quietly broken

The app could show **"Application error"** on a page that was perfectly fine —
most likely on your phone, after an update, with no way for you to clear it.

The cause: the part of the app that makes it work offline was holding on to
old program files and handing them out instead of the new ones. It's fixed,
and the app now throws away everything it had saved the next time you open it.

If you ever see that error again, tell Tyler it's the service worker and he'll
know what it means.

## Two small things to know

**Your account page** (the link at the bottom of the left-hand menu) is where
your own picture and signature live, alongside everyone else's, rather than
being tucked away somewhere separate in the admin area.

**Nothing here sends anything to a tenant without you.** The morning note goes
only to you. The clean-out list appears in a tenant's account when you start
one, and they get an email only when something they reported is marked done.
